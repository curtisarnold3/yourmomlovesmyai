Sand to Thought

How silicon becomes artificial intelligence

Every answer an AI gives you starts as a pile of quartz sand. In between sit a furnace hotter than lava, a crystal more perfect than a diamond, light focused finer than a virus, billions of switches, a building that drinks power like a small city, and a lot of arithmetic. Scroll to travel the whole chain.

Built from a short hand-drawn explainer, the “AI 101: Chips” whiteboard. When we say “the whiteboard,” we mean that original sketch, and we flag where it simplified.

CHAPTER 01

From Sand to Silicon

How does an ordinary material become the foundation of modern computing?

Stage 1 · raw material

It starts on the beach

Ordinary sand is mostly quartz, which is silicon dioxide. Silicon is the second most common element in Earth's crust, so the raw ingredient is everywhere.

The catch: here it's bound to oxygen and mixed with everything else in the ground. Chip makers favour high-purity quartz from select deposits rather than random beach sand.

Stage 2 · smelting

Pulling the oxygen out

In an electric arc furnace, carbon grabs the oxygen from quartz and leaves liquid silicon behind. This silicon is about 98–99% pure.

That's good enough for aluminium alloys and silicones. For a chip it's hopelessly dirty: roughly one stray atom in every hundred.

SiO₂ + 2C → Si + 2CO

Stage 3 · purification

Nine nines

The silicon is reacted into a liquid (trichlorosilane), distilled again and again, then decomposed back onto hot silicon rods.

What comes out is about 99.9999999% pure. That's roughly one wrong atom per billion, like a single typo across a couple of thousand novels.

Solar-grade silicon can be as low as ~6 nines. Chips need 9 or more.

Stage 4 · structure

Why silicon, of all things?

Each silicon atom has four outer electrons, and it shares them with four neighbours. In a pure crystal almost every electron is tied up, so silicon conducts poorly.

But it doesn't take much to change that. A little heat, a few deliberately added atoms, or an electric field can free up charge. That middle ground is what makes it a semiconductor.

Supporting diagram

Conductor, insulator… and the one in between

Electrons in a solid can only sit at certain energy levels. To carry current, they need to jump into the empty conduction band. The size of that jump, the band gap, decides everything.

Copper has no gap, so it always conducts. Glass has a huge gap, so it almost never does. Silicon's gap is small enough that we can open and close the door on purpose. That control is the whole trick behind every chip.

Doping is what makes it useful. Swap in roughly one phosphorus atom per million silicon atoms and you add spare electrons (n-type). Use boron and you get missing electrons, or "holes" (p-type). Chapter 4 puts the two side by side.

CHAPTER 02

The Perfect Crystal

Why does chip-making need one flawless crystal, not just pure silicon?

Pure isn't enough. The chunks from Chapter 1 are polycrystalline: millions of tiny crystals jammed together at random angles. Electrons scatter at every boundary. Chips need a single crystal where the atomic grid runs unbroken from one side of the wafer to the other.

Step 1

Melt it down

Chunks of nine-nines polysilicon go into a quartz crucible and melt at 1,414 °C. A tiny, precise amount of dopant, such as boron, is often added now so the whole crystal starts slightly p-type.

Step 2

Touch a seed

A small, perfect seed crystal is lowered until it just kisses the melt. Silicon atoms freeze onto it and copy its atomic arrangement exactly.

Step 3

Neck, then shoulder

Pulling fast at first makes a pencil-thin neck. Defects from the dip-shock grow out of the side and die there. Then the pull slows and the crystal widens.

Step 4

Grow the ingot

Over a day or more, the puller draws out a single crystal 300 mm across and often more than a metre long. Every atom in it lines up with the seed.

This is the "giant crystal" on the original whiteboard.

Step 5

Slice

A wire saw cuts the ingot into hundreds of discs at once. Each one is about as thick as a credit card (~0.8 mm).

Step 6

Polish to a mirror

Grinding, etching and chemical-mechanical polishing remove the saw damage. Lithography in Chapter 3 needs a surface this flat, because the patterns it prints are only a few dozen atoms wide.

Step 7

A blank canvas

The finished wafer is a single crystal with a mirror face. In a few months, hundreds of chips will be built on that face.

CHAPTER 03

Printing Microscopic Circuits

How do you build billions of switches on a disc the size of a dinner plate?

The whiteboard's version is a stencil, light and a lens. That's genuinely the core idea, called photolithography. But one exposure prints only one pattern on one layer. A chip is built from dozens of them, stacked with a precision of a few nanometres.

Cycle · 0

Look at a slice

We're zooming into a sliver of the wafer's surface, cut sideways so we can see the layers. The grey slab is the crystal from Chapter 2.

Cycle · 1

Deposit a film

A thin layer of material is grown or deposited across the whole wafer. Depending on the step, it's an insulator like silicon dioxide, a conductor like metal, or something else.

Cycle · 2

Coat with photoresist

A drop of light-sensitive polymer is spun into an even film on top. This is the "light-sensitive coating" from the whiteboard.

Cycle · 3

Expose through a mask

Ultraviolet light shines through a photomask (the stencil). Optics shrink its image 4× and project it onto the resist. The exposed parts change chemically.

Deep-UV scanners use 193 nm light. The newest use 13.5 nm extreme-UV.

Cycle · 4

Develop

A developer washes away the exposed resist (in a "positive" resist). Now the pattern exists as openings in the coating.

Cycle · 5

Etch

Reactive plasma eats into the film wherever the resist is open. The resist protects everything else. Alternatively, ions can be implanted through the openings to dope the silicon.

Cycle · 6

Strip and clean

The leftover resist is removed. One layer of the pattern is now permanently carved into the film.

Cycle · 7 → ∞

Now do it again. And again.

Deposit, coat, expose, develop, etch, strip, repeated layer upon layer. Each layer must line up with the one beneath it to within a few nanometres. A leading-edge chip passes through the cycle dozens of times: a thousand or more individual process steps over several months.

Supporting diagram

Inside the scanner

A lithography scanner is a camera running backwards. Light passes through the mask, and a huge lens system shrinks the image onto the wafer. The wafer stage then steps to the next exposure field (often several chips at once) and scans again, around a hundred times per wafer.

The lenses are what make "a fraction of its size" possible. Features end up far smaller than you could ever cut into a physical stencil.

EUV is different

Extreme-ultraviolet light is absorbed by glass and even by air. EUV machines therefore run in vacuum and use precision mirrors instead of lenses, with a reflective mask.

CHAPTER 04

How Transistors Work

How can a speck of silicon represent and process information?

All that printing builds one thing over and over: the transistor, a switch with no moving parts. A voltage on one terminal controls whether current can flow between the other two. Chips use pairs of these to make the ones and zeros of computing.

Interactive · required demonstration

Transistor conduction

0.00 V

The source and drain (blue-grey) are rich in free electrons (n-type). Between them, the silicon is p-type, so electrons can't cross on their own.

Put a positive voltage on the gate and its electric field pulls electrons up to the surface under the thin insulating oxide. That forms a conducting channel, and current flows.

The real device is analogue: current rises smoothly with gate voltage, as the slider shows. Digital circuits simply drive the gate to "low" or "high" and treat the result as 0 or 1. Even "off" leaks a tiny current. Today's chips wrap the gate around fin- or sheet-shaped channels for tighter control, but the principle is the same.

moving dots = electrons (current). Arrows = the gate's electric field.

Interactive · logic

Switches flipping switches

Pair an n-type transistor (on when its input is high) with a p-type one (on when its input is low) and you get CMOS logic. Here are four transistors wired as a NAND gate. The output is 0 only when both inputs are 1.

NAND is "universal": every other logic function, and therefore every adder, memory cell and processor, can be built from it.

Scale

From one switch to a processor

The whiteboard says thousands of switches fit across a human hair. That holds up: a hair is about 70–100 micrometres wide, and transistor gates now repeat every ~50 nanometres or so.

CHAPTER 05

CPU vs. GPU vs. ASIC

If every chip is made of transistors, why are there different kinds?

Because how you arrange billions of transistors decides what a chip is good at. Spend them on clever control logic and big caches, and you get a CPU. Spend them on thousands of simple arithmetic units, and you get a GPU. Hard-wire them for one kind of maths, and you get an ASIC. AI happens to be mostly one kind of maths, repeated a staggering number of times.

Interactive · workload comparison

Give all three chips the same job

Pick a workload and press run. Each tile is a processing unit, and lit tiles are busy. The progress bars count conceptual time steps. These are illustrations of behaviour, not benchmarks of any real product.

CPU

A few powerful cores with big control logic and caches (plus some vector units)

GPU

Thousands of simpler cores that execute the same instruction in lockstep

ASIC (e.g. a TPU)

A hard-wired grid of multiply-add cells that data flows through

What to take away

Match the machine to the work

CPUs excel at unpredictable, step-by-step logic. They aren't strictly one-thing-at-a-time: modern CPUs have many cores and vector units. In an AI server, the CPU is the coordinator that feeds and directs everything else.

GPUs were built to colour millions of pixels at once. Neural networks turned out to need the same pattern: huge piles of identical, independent multiply-adds. GPUs are still programmable, which is why they dominate. But they stumble on branchy, sequential code.

ASICs such as Google's TPUs give up flexibility for efficiency on one family of operations. If the work fits, they're excellent. If it doesn't, they do it poorly or hand it back to the host CPU.

From the original whiteboard

The kitchen analogy

The source reel compares the CPU to a brilliant head chef who cooks anything, one plate at a time. The GPU is an army of line cooks all chopping at once. The ASIC is a waffle iron: it makes exactly one thing, perfectly.

A useful picture, with three fixes. The chef actually has a few sous-chefs, since CPUs are multi-core. The waffle iron can manage a little more than waffles, since TPUs carry small general-purpose units too. And the line cooks have to all do the same motion together; ask them to each cook something different and most of them stand idle. Workload A above shows exactly that.

CHAPTER 06

Inside a Computing Node

What else does a processor need before it can do any work?

A chip on its own does nothing. It needs data delivered fast enough, somewhere to keep it, a way to talk to its neighbours, clean power and constant cooling. An AI server, called a node, packs all of that into one tray. The whiteboard's recipe was "CPUs + GPUs or ASICs + memory and network." Here's what that looks like when it's built.

CHAPTER 07

Exploring an AI Data Center

How do thousands of nodes become one machine?

Nodes stack into racks, and racks stand in rows. Tightly networked groups of racks form a pod or cluster that can train or serve a single giant model. Around them sits the rest of the building: substations, generators, switches, pipes and cooling towers. In practice, a data center is mostly a machine for moving electricity in and heat out.

Interactive · infrastructure layers

From the source reels: "a pod is the big connected cluster… each rack consists of individual nodes… each node contains the chips." One correction: a row holds many racks. A rack is a single cabinet about 2 m tall.

CHAPTER 08

From Question to Answer

What actually happens, physically and mathematically, when you ask an AI something?

Two very different things meet here. The model is mathematics: a fixed recipe of matrix operations plus billions of numbers ("weights") learned in training. The hardware is the physical machinery from Chapters 1 to 7 that carries out that arithmetic. There is no brain in the building, only numbers flowing through transistors.

1 · input

You type a question

Your text leaves your phone as ordinary bytes and travels over the internet to a data center like the one in Chapter 7. A load balancer hands it to a server with a copy of the model loaded.

2 · tokens

Text becomes tokens

A tokenizer chops the text into common pieces (whole words, word fragments, punctuation). Each piece is an ID number in a fixed vocabulary of tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand entries.

The ID numbers shown are illustrative.

3 · vectors

Tokens become lists of numbers

Each ID looks up an embedding: a long list of numbers (thousands of them) that the model learned. Similar meanings end up with similar lists. From here on, everything is arithmetic.

4 · the model

Through the layers

The vectors pass through dozens of identical-looking transformer layers. In attention, each token looks back at the earlier tokens to gather context ("blue" → "sky"). Then a feed-forward step transforms each one. Under the hood, both are mostly huge matrix multiplications. The panel right after this sequence shows what that means.

5 · the hardware

Where the arithmetic physically happens

Those matrix multiplications are exactly Workload B from Chapter 5. The weights stream out of HBM (high-bandwidth memory, stacked right beside each GPU) into thousands of GPU cores. Big models are split across many GPUs. Within a node they trade partial results over NVLink every layer. Across nodes they pass results over the network between groups of layers.

Mathematics: violet. Physical hardware and data movement: cyan.

6 · prediction

A probability for every possible next token

The final layer turns the last position into a score for every token in the vocabulary. The system picks one, often the likeliest, sometimes with a little randomness.

Probabilities shown are illustrative.

7 · repeat

One token at a time

The chosen token is appended and the model runs again to get the next one. It doesn't redo all its work: what it already computed about earlier tokens is kept in a cache (the "KV cache"), so each new pass processes just the newest token through every layer. A 300-word answer still means hundreds of passes, each reading all or a large share of the model's weights.

8 · output

The answer comes home

Tokens are turned back into text and streamed to your screen as they're generated. That's why answers appear a few characters (one token) at a time. The by-product, as the whiteboard says, is heat. Every token produced was paid for in electricity and carried away by the cooling plant.

Interactive · what “matrix multiplication” means

Where numbers become a word

Take the last token's list of numbers (left). The model holds a grid of weights, numbers learned in training. To get each output number, multiply every input by its weight in that column and add the results. Each multiply-then-add is one multiply-add, the exact operation of Workload B in Chapter 5. Inside the chip it's done by multiplier and adder circuits built from the logic gates of Chapter 4.

A real layer does this with thousands of inputs and thousands of columns, for every token, in every layer. That's trillions of multiply-adds per answer.

The demo shows that very last step: the same operation against a table with one column per vocabulary word (three shown here). Each column's result is that token's score. The highest score marks the most likely next word, and the sampler usually, but not always, picks it. Sunlight leads simply because its column produced the biggest number.

Tiny illustrative numbers. Real models use thousands of dimensions.

The whole chain

An AI answer is the last link in a very long physical chain.

Quartz turned into nine-nines silicon, grown into one flawless crystal, then sliced and polished. Light printed dozens of layers of switches on it. Billions of switches, arranged for parallel arithmetic, were wired into nodes, racks and power-hungry buildings. Those buildings run a mathematical model one token at a time.

Every answer is a little of all of it. You'll never read one the same way again.
