Who actually makes AI chips?

Inside the global supply chain behind AI computing

Nvidia doesn't make its chips any more than Apple makes the iPhone. Follow one AI processor from a customer's order to a finished rack, and meet the dozens of specialists who each build one piece of it.

01

The AI chip ecosystem

What companies actually make up the AI semiconductor industry?

Nvidia's CEO likes to describe AI as a five-layer cake: energy at the bottom, then chips, then the infrastructure that houses them, then the models, and finally the applications we actually touch.

This story lives in layer two: chips. And the first thing to know is that "chips" means two different families.

Logic chips do the thinking. They run the math. Nvidia is the poster child, with AMD and Broadcom as the other big AI logic designers.

Memory chips hold the data the logic is chewing on. They're a separate industry, and unlike Nvidia, the big memory makers mostly build their chips in their own factories. As we'll see in Chapter 8, an AI processor is useless without a very special kind of memory sitting right next to it.

Five layers, one dependency chain

Every layer rests on the one below it. Models can't train without infrastructure, infrastructure is empty without chips, and chips draw on enormous amounts of energy. Pick a chip family to look closer.

02

The chip order

What happens when an AI infrastructure customer orders a chip?
Fig 2.1 · an order becomes a specification

Picture a company like CoreWeave (a data-center operator) building capacity for a customer like OpenAI. It needs processors, so it orders from a chip company like Nvidia or AMD. The biggest cloud and AI companies also commission custom chips, often co-designed with Broadcom.

Here's the twist. None of those three owns the factories that will build the chip. They're "fabless": their product is the design. Manufacturing goes out to specialists.

Nvidia

Designs GPUs and the boards, networking and software around them, then sells complete systems.

AMD

Designs GPUs and CPUs for data centers; also fabless since spinning off its factories in 2009.

Broadcom

Co-designs custom AI accelerators (ASICs) with large cloud companies, alongside its networking chips.

Business models differ, but all three share one move: they hand a finished design to somebody else's factory.

03

Designing the chip

How does an engineer design a modern processor?
Fig 3.1 · functional block diagram
Explore the chip design

The order lands on an engineering team, and the first thing they reach for is software. Modern AI processors hold tens of billions of transistors. Nobody places those by hand.

The tools are called EDA, for electronic design automation. If you've ever used CAD to design a part, it's the same idea scaled up to absurdity. Two American companies dominate the market: USCadence and USSynopsys (Siemens EDA is a smaller third). Chip designers typically license tools from both.

EDA carries a design through several translations. Engineers describe what the chip should do (big functional blocks), then how it behaves (logic), then where every piece physically sits (layout). Software checks timing, power and manufacturing rules at every stage, because a single error can cost months.

The output is a set of geometric files, one pattern per manufacturing layer, built against the factory's own rulebook (its process design kit). That file set is what travels to the factory. This hand-off is often called tape-out.

The first thing that leaves the designer isn't a chip. It's information.

04

The silicon wafer

Where does the physical chip begin?

The design goes to a foundry, a contract chip factory. But the foundry needs something to print on: a polished silicon wafer, a disc about 300 mm across (roughly a dinner plate) sliced from a single crystal of ultra-pure silicon.

Two Japanese companies, JPShin-Etsu and JPSUMCO, supply about half the world's silicon wafers. Both trade in Tokyo, which is why you won't find them in US semiconductor funds.

05

Inside the fab

How does a foundry transform a wafer into integrated circuits?

The factory is called a fab. For the most advanced logic chips, that mostly means TWTSMC (Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company), which makes roughly 90% of the world's leading-edge chips. That concentration is a big reason Taiwan has become so important geopolitically.

Inside, wafers ride sealed pods along overhead rails between machines made by other companies. TSMC runs the process; it buys the tools.

06

Lithography up close

How can microscopic patterns be transferred onto a silicon wafer?
Fig 6.1 · EUV scanner cutaway schematic
Follow the pattern

Lithography is the printing step, and it's the hardest. The wafer, now coated with light-sensitive resist, goes into a machine where light bounces off a series of mirrors and projects the circuit pattern onto the coating.

For the smallest features, that light is extreme ultraviolet (EUV), at 13.5 nm wavelength. EUV is absorbed by almost everything, including air and glass, so the whole path runs in vacuum and uses mirrors instead of lenses, and even the pattern master (the reticle, or mask) is a mirror.

Only one company makes leading-edge EUV machines: NLASML, based in the Netherlands. Older, less advanced lithography using deep-ultraviolet light is made by several companies.

Each machine prints one small field at a time, stepping across the wafer, die by die. The light only marks which parts of the resist will survive development, and that decides where the circuit ends up.

Scale, honestly

Each labelled mark is 1,000× smaller than the one before; each tick is 10×. A die is millimeters across; the finest patterned features are measured in tens of nanometers, thousands of times thinner than a human hair.

07

From wafer to finished die

How are individual integrated circuits separated and checked?

Testing is often described as "the last step." In reality it's been happening all along. USKLA makes inspection and measurement tools that check wafers as layers are built. When every layer is finished, the wafer gets electrically probed, cut apart, and the survivors are tested again.

Defects, die count and pass/fail pattern here are invented for teaching. Real yields are closely guarded and vary by product, process maturity and die size. Big dies are hit harder: one particle can ruin a large die that would only have taken out a small one.

08

Advanced packaging and HBM

How does a processor become a high-performance AI computing package?

A bare GPU is fast but starved. It needs data delivered at enormous rates, which is why it gets mounted side by side with high-bandwidth memory (HBM): towers of DRAM chips stacked vertically and wired through their own silicon.

USMicron and KRSK Hynix are two of the major HBM makers (Samsung is the third). Unlike Nvidia, they're not fabless: they design and manufacture the memory in their own fabs, stack it, and ship finished HBM to TSMC for packaging. The memory stacks and the logic dies sit on an interposer (a full slice of silicon on older GPUs) laced with dense wiring, so data travels millimeters instead of centimeters. For Nvidia's chips, TSMC does this step too, with a packaging process called CoWoS. Its current Blackwell GPUs use the CoWoS-L variant: two GPU dies and eight HBM stacks joined through small silicon bridges embedded in the interposer.

Under that sits the package substrate, which fans thousands of tiny connections out to something a circuit board can handle. Its insulating build-up layers are made from JPAjinomoto's ABF film, from the company best known for MSG. ABF is one material inside the substrate, not the whole substrate.

The "chip" you buy is a small city: one or two logic dies, a ring of memory towers, an interposer, a substrate and thousands of solder joints, from at least four different companies.

From chip to compute tray

What turns a packaged processor into a usable computing subsystem?

Here's what most people miss: what Nvidia sells is less a chip than a whole system design. TSMC ships the finished packages onward, and a Taiwanese manufacturer like TWFoxconn or TWWistron mounts them onto Nvidia's board designs and builds them into a compute tray, then a rack. 10

The complete AI rack

Why is the finished commercial AI system much more than a processor?
Fig 10.1 · rack-scale AI system

Stack the trays into a cabinet, add switch trays that let every GPU talk to every other GPU, power shelves and a busbar to feed them, and liquid cooling to carry the heat away. The result is a single rack that behaves like one giant computer.

The original explainer puts the price of one such rack at around $6 million. Treat that as a ballpark: Nvidia publishes no list price. Analyst estimates put a GB200 NVL72 rack near $3 million and the newer GB300 version around $6 million.
