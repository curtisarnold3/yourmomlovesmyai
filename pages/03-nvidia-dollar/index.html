Tracing $1 of Nvidia Sales

How a chip company became financially connected to the entire AI economy

In a whiteboard reel, a finance creator follows a single dollar of Nvidia's revenue from the customer's wire transfer all the way into OpenAI's cap table, a nuclear-site data center in Ohio and a $500 billion pact with Wall Street. We rebuilt her math from Nvidia's own filings and found that most of it holds. The interesting part is where the dollar stops being a dollar.

CHAPTER 01
Where does the dollar come from?

Who pays Nvidia?

Nvidia doesn't really sell chips anymore. It sells racks: cabinets of GPUs, networking and liquid cooling assembled by partners like Foxconn and Wistron, which the reel says run about $6 million apiece.

So who's writing those checks? The reel splits a dollar of sales three ways. About half comes from the hyperscalers: Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta and Oracle, plus the frontier labs renting from them. Almost all the rest is what Nvidia now calls ACIE (AI clouds, industrial and enterprise). That covers neoclouds like CoreWeave, sovereign AI projects and companies building AI in-house.

And gaming, the business Nvidia was built on? It's now inside a segment called Edge Computing, and it brings in about 8 cents.

The company famous for gaming GPUs now earns 92 cents of every dollar from data centers.

This is the one chapter where the reel's numbers line up with Nvidia's own filing almost to the cent: the 10-Q's six-month split is 51.6 / 40.8 / 7.6. (One caveat: Nvidia moved one customer from ACIE to Hyperscale this year and restated its first quarter to match.)

CHAPTER 02
How does revenue become reported profit?

What happens to $1?

Here's the first place our dollar changes shape. Profit isn't a pile of money that gets carved off the dollar. It's a calculation.

Start with $1.00 of sales. Take out 25 cents for making the chips and about 9 cents for engineers, research and overhead. That leaves about 66 cents of operating profit, a margin most companies can only dream about.

Then something unusual. Nvidia adds 13 cents of gains on its investments, mostly because stakes it owns in other companies went up in value. Taxes take 13 cents back out. The bottom line is 66 cents of reported net income. Same number as the operating profit, but only by coincidence: the gains and the taxes happen to cancel out.

About one-sixth of Nvidia's pre-tax profit this half came from its stock portfolio going up, not from selling chips.

Hold on to that 13 cents. It's real profit under the accounting rules, but no customer paid it. That matters in the next chapter.

CHAPTER 03
Why is $0.66 of profit only $0.42 of cash?

Profit is not cash

Picture a lemonade stand that sells $100 of lemonade on credit, prepays $40 for next week's lemons, and sees the lemon-futures it owns rise $13. On paper, a $100-plus day. In the cash box, it's down $40.

Nvidia's version: of 66 cents of profit, only 42 cents arrived as cash from operations. The biggest leaks were customers who hadn't paid yet (receivables up 14 cents), inventory piling up (6 cents) and prepayments to lock in supply (4 cents). Then there's the 13 cents of investment gains. Most of them (about 84%) were paper gains on stakes Nvidia still holds, and they never touched the bank account.

Two things helped (+8 cents): Nvidia hadn't yet paid all its bills and taxes, and some customers paid in advance. And some expenses, like stock compensation and depreciation, cost no cash at all (+4 cents).

Profit is a story told by accounting rules. Cash is what's actually in the account.

If you add the reel's rounded numbers, they total 41 cents, not 42. The filing's unrounded figures land on 41.8. We show that 1-cent gap as a labelled rounding residual instead of quietly stretching a bar.

CHAPTER 04
How much cash is there to spend?

How much cash is available?

Nvidia generated 42 cents of operating cash per dollar of sales, and then borrowed more anyway.

In its second quarter Nvidia issued about $25 billion of bonds, which is 14 cents on our scale. It also sold about 7 cents of securities it already owned. Neither one is income. Borrowing has to be paid back, and selling securities just swaps one asset for another, the way cashing a savings bond doesn't make you richer.

Add it up and Nvidia had 63 cents of new cash to work with. The reel follows it three ways:

26 cents went back to shareholders as buybacks and dividends. 24 cents was invested in other AI companies. The last 13 cents was kept or spent on itself: data-center equipment, taxes on employee stock awards, a $2.9 billion Groq payment and a bigger cash cushion.

For every dollar it sold, Nvidia bought about 24 cents of stakes in other AI companies. That's roughly 20 cents net of the stakes it sold.

CHAPTER 05
What kinds of companies does Nvidia invest in?

Nvidia's investment ecosystem

Jensen Huang describes AI as a five-layer cake: energy, chips, infrastructure, models and applications. Nvidia sells into layer two. Its money now reaches all five.

Most of the dollars sit in the models layer. A $30 billion pledge to OpenAI, paid in tranches through 2026, is the biggest equity investment Nvidia has ever made. It sits alongside stakes in Anthropic, xAI and a handful of younger labs.

In its own chip layer, Nvidia bought into the companies it depends on, and one it competes with. Intel makes CPUs that pair with Nvidia GPUs. Coherent and Lumentum make the lasers that move data between GPUs. Synopsys makes the software chips are designed in. Marvell designs custom AI chips for Amazon and others, meaning the very chips meant to replace Nvidia's.

In infrastructure it backs neoclouds like CoreWeave and Nebius, which buy Nvidia GPUs and rent them out. In energy, it took a stake in SB Energy, the developer of OpenAI's planned Ohio campus.

Not every line is ownership

Purple lines are equity stakes. Coral dashed lines are guarantees. Pale nodes are companies the reel lists but whose deal size we couldn't confirm. Nvidia's 10-Q doesn't name its investees, so amounts come from company announcements.

CHAPTER 06
How does the portfolio change over time?

The investment portfolio grows

The cents change meaning here

Until now, each cent was a slice of money flowing through six months of business. From here on, the cents measure things Nvidia holds or has promised at a moment in time, divided by six months of sales just to give a sense of scale. They didn't come out of the sales dollar. Against annualized sales, each figure would be roughly half.

At the start of 2026, Nvidia held stakes worth about 22 cents per dollar of sales. By the end of July they were worth 56 cents, about $99 billion, or 31% of everything Nvidia owns.

Two forces drove that. Nvidia put in 24 cents of new money, and the stakes it already held went up in value. The reel calls the second part 10 cents of appreciation.

The filing shows something a little different. Valuations rose about 13 cents, but Nvidia also sold about 4 cents of stock along the way. The reel's 10 cents is roughly those two netted together (13 − 4 is really 9.3). Same ending number, two different motions.

A stake that went up 13 cents is worth more. It's still not 13 cents of cash until someone buys it.

Terms: public stakes are carried at market value (today's share price). Private stakes are carried at carrying value: cost, adjusted when a new funding round reprices them. Neither is cash.

Concentration is the other story. More than half the portfolio sits in frontier labs, and the reel says over half of that is OpenAI. In the supply chain, the reel puts Intel at about 70%. In neoclouds, CoreWeave is about half. Nvidia's filing names no investees, so these shares are the reel's estimates.

CHAPTER 07
What's the difference between investing and promising?

The promises beyond the cash

Writing a check and co-signing a lease are not the same thing. The co-signer pays nothing unless the tenant stops paying, but the co-signer is still on the hook.

The reel says Nvidia has promised support worth $1.23 per dollar of sales, about twice the value of its stakes. The reel doesn't show its working, but we found a combination of items in Nvidia's filing that adds up to exactly $1.23. There's a catch: it only gets there by counting $29 billion of cloud computing Nvidia buys for its own research, which isn't support for anyone. Without that, the promises come to about $1.07.

The biggest by far: in August 2026 Nvidia signed guarantees capped at $105 billion (the press calls them residual-value guarantees) on parts of the lease and power payments for OpenAI's planned campus in Pike County, Ohio, which SoftBank's SB Energy is developing. If OpenAI ever stops paying, Nvidia covers the gap between a guaranteed value and whatever re-leasing or selling the site recovers. If OpenAI keeps paying, Nvidia pays nothing. And none of it switches on until each lease starts, with the first of nine phases expected in Nvidia's fiscal 2029. No guarantee is in force yet, so today's exposure is close to zero, even though the 10-Q reports the full $105 billion as the maximum.

Next come neocloud-related items: $36 billion of AI-cloud capacity agreements, $20 billion of leases Nvidia signed and plans to hand to others, and $3.5 billion of land and power guarantees. Separately, there's $25 billion of future equity checks to AI model makers, infrastructure financiers and other private companies, which may include the last $10 billion of the OpenAI pledge.

Terms: a residual-value guarantee promises a lender or landlord that an asset will be worth at least a set amount; the guarantor pays only the shortfall. A gross cap is the most that could ever be paid, not a forecast.

What we couldn't confirm

The reel calls the Ohio site 9.2 gigawatts and half a trillion dollars. The filing covers about 4.25 GW of IT load with an option for about 3.8 GW more (~8 GW). 9.2 GW may be total facility power, but neither that nor the "half trillion" appears in the filing. The guarantee was also signed in August, after the six-month period the rest of the dollar measures.

CHAPTER 08
Why can exposure exceed a dollar per dollar of sales?

How much is Nvidia exposed to?

Stack it all up and the reel's punchline arrives: for every $1 of sales, $1.79 is invested or promised, and up to $2.49 is on the hook.

The extra 70 cents comes from August's memoranda of understanding (MOUs) with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR. These are non-binding letters of intent: together the firms hope to raise more than $500 billion over time to finance AI buildouts, and Nvidia said it may provide residual-value support for up to 25% of a given deal, case by case. Apply 25% to the whole $500 billion and you get $125 billion, or 70 cents. That's an illustration. Nvidia didn't disclose any total cap.

Using our cleaner reading from Chapter 7 (leaving out Nvidia's own cloud purchases), the stack comes to $1.62 and $2.32 instead.

The reel jokes that next to Lehman Brothers' roughly 31-to-1 leverage, "we're okay." Lehman really did report about 31× (total assets divided by equity). But that's a different ratio. Measured the same way, Nvidia's leverage in July 2026 was about 1.4×. The $2.49 divides assets plus promises plus an illustration by six months of revenue, so it can't be set against Lehman's number.

CHAPTER 09
What does this say about Nvidia's place in AI?

The chip company and the financial network

Zoom in on one product and Nvidia looks like a chip company. Its customers buy racks (cyan), and some of them sell the computing back to Nvidia (teal).

Pull back and the same customers are also investees. Nvidia owns pieces of OpenAI, CoreWeave and Nebius (violet), and of the suppliers it buys from (amber cash).

Pull back further and promises appear: guarantees behind an Ohio campus and AI-cloud leases (coral, dashed), capacity agreements and equity commitments (amber, dashed), and non-binding memoranda with six Wall Street firms (coral, dotted).

The reel's conclusion: this chip maker looks more like a venture or hedge fund invested across the whole stack. That's an interpretation, not an accounting category. What the filings do show is a company whose customers, suppliers, investees and guarantee beneficiaries increasingly overlap.

Filter relationship types

To understand Nvidia, you have to follow more than its chip revenue. Follow its financial and commercial relationships across the AI economy too.

None of this says whether those relationships are wise or risky. A guarantee that's never called costs nothing. A stake in a company that IPOs at a higher price is a windfall. The same web that spreads demand for Nvidia's chips also ties Nvidia's fortunes to its customers' ability to pay. That's what the dollar shows: a chip sale that also leaves Nvidia owning a piece of its customers, and promising things to them.
