How model weights, inference, and compute are reshaping the AI industry

Every large language model ends its training the same way: as a very large file of numbers. What a company does with that one file, lock it in a vault or post it for anyone to download, decides who can run it, who gets paid, and which chips light up when you ask it a question.

Act I

The landscape

Before we open up a model, let's find where models live in the AI economy, and who makes them.

01
Orientation

A five-layer cake

Where do AI models sit in the economy?

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang likes to describe AI as a five-layer cake: energy at the bottom, then chips, then the data-center infrastructure that rents out compute, then the models, and finally the applications we actually touch.

Nvidia is the poster child for layer two. Anthropic and OpenAI are the poster children for layer four. But here's the twist the original whiteboard flagged with a question mark: Nvidia makes models too.

Tap any layer. Earlier episodes in this series covered layers 1–3.

02
The players

Who releases what?

Openness is a decision made model by model, not company by company.

The original whiteboard sorted companies into three camps. Frontier labs mostly keep their weights closed. Hyperscalers do both. Chinese labs mostly publish their weights. That's a useful first sketch, and it mostly holds up in November 2026. But zoom in and almost every company is running two strategies at once: a closed flagship it charges for, and smaller or older open models it gives away.

03
Vocabulary

“Open weights” isn’t “open source”

What do you actually get when a model is called open?

The recording says Nvidia's models are "open source." In everyday speech, sure. But there's a distinction worth keeping straight, because people make expensive decisions on it.

A model has several separable ingredients: the trained weights, the code that runs them, the training code, and the training data. "Open weights" means you can download the first one. You may or may not get the rest, and the weights arrive with a license that can range from "do anything" to "no commercial use."

Open weights let you: run the model on hardware you choose, fine-tune it on your own data, inspect its numbers, and keep using a version forever even if the maker moves on.
Open weights don't automatically give you: the training data, a guarantee of unrestricted commercial use, or the ability to retrain the model from scratch.
Act II

Inside the model

To see why a file of numbers is worth billions, we have to watch one being made, starting from a single sentence.

Start with text

Training data is mostly text: web pages, books, code, conversations. Our whole story will follow one tiny sentence through the process.

Chop it into tokens

A tokenizer splits text into pieces from a fixed vocabulary. Common words usually become one token (often with their leading space attached); rare words get split into fragments like sleep + ing. Production vocabularies hold roughly 100,000 to 250,000 pieces.

Each token has an ID

Every piece in the vocabulary has a number, its token ID. The ID is just a label, like a locker number. Token 5679 isn't "bigger" or "more" than token 374 in any meaningful way.

These IDs come from our toy vocabulary, not a real tokenizer.

The ID opens a locker

The ID points to one row of an embedding table. That row is a list of numbers called a vector, and unlike the ID, those numbers are learned during training. The ID is the address; the vector is what lives there.

We draw 8 numbers per token. Real models use thousands per token.

Vectors flow into the network

The sequence of vectors enters the model's layers, where they get mixed, compared and transformed by matrix math. Everything the model does from here on is arithmetic on numbers like these.

Try it: toy tokenizer

What's real here: the idea of splitting text into a fixed vocabulary of pieces, and of mapping each piece to an ID and then to a learned vector.

What's simplified: our splitting rules, IDs and vector values are made up for illustration. Real tokenizers (byte-pair encoding and relatives) learn their vocabulary from data, and real vectors are much longer.

05
Parameters

One knob, then billions

What exactly is a "weight"?
Error (sum of squared misses)
–
Best possible β for this data
?

Illustrative data: 120 simulated daily returns for a stock with a "true" beta of 1.3. Not real market data.

The recording reaches for a finance-nerd analogy, and it's a good one. Plot a stock's daily returns against the S&P 500's. Draw the best line through the cloud. Its slope is the stock's beta: one number, learned from data, that turns an input (market move) into a prediction (stock move).

That slope is a parameter. "Fitting" means turning the knob until the line misses the dots by as little as possible.

A language model is that idea pushed to an absurd extreme. Instead of y = βx + α, each layer computes something like y = Wx + b, where W is a whole grid of knobs. Stack enough grids and you reach billions or trillions of parameters, the words "parameters" and "weights" being used interchangeably.

Where the analogy stops. A beta is one linear knob you can read and interpret. A model's weights interact through many nonlinear layers, so no single weight "means" anything on its own. And regression has a neat formula for the best fit; a neural network has to search for good weights step by step.
06
Training

Nudging the knobs

How does random noise turn into something that predicts language?

When training begins, every weight is random noise. The model's guesses are garbage. Then a loop runs, over and over: show the model some text, let it guess the next token, measure how wrong it was, and nudge every weight a tiny bit in whichever direction would have made it less wrong. That last step, computing the direction for every weight at once, is called backpropagation, and the nudging is gradient descent.

Below is a real neural network training live in your browser. It's microscopic, 90 parameters learning from 7 sentences, but the loop is the same one that, at vastly larger scale, produced every model in this story.

Notice the probabilities don't go to 100%. "The dog is" is followed by "sleeping" in some sentences and "at" in another, so the model learns a distribution. Each training example says "the answer was X" with 100% certainty; the model learns how often, across many examples, each answer shows up.
Scale check. Our model has 90 parameters and trains in seconds on 7 sentences. Frontier training runs adjust hundreds of billions to trillions of parameters over many trillions of tokens, on thousands of accelerators for weeks or months. The shape of the loop is the same; the bill is not.
07
Meaning as geometry

King − man + woman ≈ queen

Do learned numbers actually capture meaning?

Hand-placed 2-D illustration. Real word vectors have hundreds of dimensions; any flat picture of them is a projection that distorts distances.

In 2013, a team at Google led by Tomas Mikolov trained word vectors on a large text corpus and noticed something uncanny. Take the vector for king, subtract man, add woman, and the nearest word to where you land is queen.

Nobody programmed "royalty" or "gender" into those numbers. The directions emerged because words used in similar contexts get nudged toward similar vectors during training. Meaning, at least some slice of it, shows up as geometry.

The fine print: the trick works for some relationships and fails for many others; the classic demo excludes the input words when searching for the nearest neighbor; and modern LLMs build context-dependent representations, so "bank" near "river" ends up somewhere different from "bank" near "loan." The 2013 result is a window into the idea, not a description of how a 2026 model works internally.

08
The central object

The frozen file

What's left when training ends?

When training stops, the weights stop moving. They're frozen and saved. That saved set of numbers is the checkpoint, and it's the single most valuable object in this whole story. It's what a lab spends hundreds of millions or billions of dollars of compute to produce.

Physically, it's unglamorous: a folder of large files full of numbers, plus a few small files describing the architecture and the tokenizer. Hold on to this image. In a few chapters, the exact same folder will either stay locked in a vault or get posted for the world.

A TYPICAL OPEN-WEIGHT RELEASE FOLDER (representative layout)
INSIDE ONE SHARD: NAMED GRIDS OF NUMBERS
Weights are not the training data. A model trained on trillions of words doesn't store a copy of them; the checkpoint is far smaller than its training set. That said, models can memorize and reproduce some passages they saw often, which is why "it's just numbers" isn't the whole story either.
File size ≈ parameters × bytes per parameter. A 120-billion-parameter model stored at 16-bit precision (2 bytes each) is about 240 GB. Store each weight in 4 bits instead and it shrinks to about 60 GB. We'll come back to this; it decides where a model can run.
Act III

Out into the world

A frozen checkpoint does nothing on its own. It earns its keep when someone asks it a question. That's where open and closed finally part ways.

09
Two different jobs

Training builds it. Inference uses it.

What happens when you hit "send"?

Inference is the model doing its job: you hand it a prompt, and it runs the frozen weights forward to predict one next token. Then it appends that token to the text and predicts again. And again. Every word you've ever watched stream out of a chatbot appeared this way, one token at a time.

A claim worth sharpening. The recording says inference chips "don't have to be nearly as sophisticated" as training chips. Partly true: inference skips the backward pass and can tolerate lower numerical precision, which is why Google (TPU), Amazon (Inferentia for inference; Trainium, built for training but also used for serving), Microsoft (Maia), Meta (MTIA) and OpenAI (with Broadcom) have all built or announced their own chips. But serving big models fast is its own hard engineering problem, and a lot of frontier inference still runs on top-end GPUs. "Different," more than "simpler."

Distribution

Same file, two destinies

Where does the checkpoint go after training?

Here is the fork the whole episode has been building toward. Follow the cyan request as it travels each path. Notice the violet checkpoint is identical on both sides. What differs is the boundary around it.

Follow

Closed weights

The maker never hands over the file. You pay the maker, or one of its licensed partners, for access by the token or by subscription. Claude, for example, is available through Anthropic's own API and also through Amazon Bedrock, Google Cloud's Vertex AI and Microsoft Foundry. Closed doesn't mean "one cloud." It means the weights stay under the maker's control, and the maker decides what hardware serves them.

Whiteboard detail: Anthropic runs Claude on a mix of Google TPUs, AWS Trainium and Nvidia GPUs. OpenAI runs on Nvidia GPUs, has a multi-gigawatt AMD deal starting in late 2026, and is co-designing its own inference chip with Broadcom (the whiteboard's "Jalapeño").

Open weights

The maker posts the file, usually on Hugging Face. A developer downloads it and needs two more things: compute (their own GPU, a Mac, or rented cloud servers) and serving software such as vLLM, SGLang, llama.cpp, Ollama or Nvidia's TensorRT-LLM to turn the file into a fast, working endpoint.

Whiteboard steps, preserved: 1. Hugging Face → 2. Compute → 3. "Serving software," e.g. vLLM.

Closed: you rent answers. Open: you get the engine, and you have to find the fuel and the garage.
11
Memory math

Will it fit?

Why can you run some open models on a laptop, but not others?

To answer a prompt quickly, a model's weights need to sit in fast memory, usually the GPU's. So the first question about any open model is brutally simple: does the file fit? The original whiteboard plotted parameters against memory and drew a line at roughly 100 GB: below it, you can often run locally; above it, you typically rent a data center.

Bytes per weight

Weights only. Running a model also needs working memory for the conversation (the "KV cache") and the software itself, often adding 10–30% or much more for very long prompts. Correcting the rule of thumb. The recording says ~100 GB "typically supports around 100 billion parameters." That's true at 8-bit precision (1 byte per weight). At 16-bit, 100B parameters need ~200 GB; squeezed to 4-bit, ~50 GB. Precision is the hidden third axis of this chart. OpenAI's gpt-oss-120b, for example, ships most of its weights in a ~4-bit format precisely so it fits on a single 80 GB GPU.
Bigger isn't automatically smarter. More parameters tends to mean more capability, but training data, training method and architecture matter enormously. Google says its 31B Gemma 4 beats much larger models on some leaderboards. And "mixture-of-experts" models like Kimi K3 store 2.8 trillion parameters but use only ~104 billion per token: all of them must sit in memory, yet each answer touches a small slice.
12
Deployment

On your desk or in the cloud

What changes when the model runs on your machine?

Local

  • Upfront hardware cost, then roughly free per question (plus electricity).
  • Prompts can stay on the machine: a strong privacy and compliance property, if the surrounding apps, plugins and telemetry also stay local.
  • Works offline; nobody can change or retire the model on you.
  • Limited to models that fit (Chapter 11), usually at lower speed than a data center.

Cloud

  • Pay per token or per GPU-hour; scale up and down with demand.
  • The only practical option for the biggest models: a 2.8T-parameter model needs over a terabyte of fast memory even at 4-bit.
  • Open models run in the cloud too: hyperscalers, neoclouds and inference startups all host them.
  • Data leaves your machine, so contracts and provider controls carry the privacy burden.
Three myths, retired. (1) "Open means local." Plenty of open-weight usage runs in someone else's cloud. (2) "Local means private." Only if every piece of software around the model behaves. (3) "Closed means one cloud." Closed models are sold through multiple partners; what's closed is the file, not the venue.

Economics

Free model, paid compute

Who gets paid when you ask a question?

The recording offers a sharp explanation for why Google and Alibaba give models away: they make money on compute. A free model that runs best on your cloud is a funnel into your cloud. And every model, open or closed, has to run on something, which is the part Nvidia sells.

Layer 2 meets layer 4

Why a chipmaker wants open models

What is Nvidia doing on both sides of the cake?

Nvidia's biggest customers, Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta and OpenAI, are all building their own chips, especially for inference. That's the threat. If the most-used models are closed and served only inside those companies' clouds, more of the world's inference could quietly migrate onto in-house silicon.

Open models change that math. Anyone, anywhere, can run them, and the default place to run almost anything is still an Nvidia GPU with Nvidia's software. So Nvidia has moved up the cake on three fronts:

15
Competition

The switching question

Is open-weight AI a threat to Anthropic and OpenAI?

The recording ends on its thesis: as open-weight models get better and easier to run, the biggest competitive threat to closed labs is customers switching. That's an argument, not a fact, so here are both sides as their advocates would make them.

The case that switching pressure is real

  • The gap is narrowing. In July–August 2026, Kimi K3 (2.8T) and Qwen3.8-Max (2.4T) shipped open weights; Alibaba's own announcement claimed Qwen3.8 was "second only to Fable 5."
  • Switching is cheap in code: most serving software exposes the same style of API many apps already call, so swapping the model behind an app can be a configuration change.
  • Open models give control over cost, data location and model versions, which regulated buyers value.
  • Companies with something else to sell (cloud, chips, ads) can afford to give strong models away.

The case that closed labs keep their edge

  • Benchmarks aren't the job. Closed labs argue their models still lead on long, messy, real-world tasks, and vendor benchmark claims often await independent checks.
  • Running a 2-trillion-parameter model well is hard and expensive; a managed API hides that cost and effort.
  • Products, safety testing, enterprise contracts and support are part of what customers buy.
  • Anthropic argues capable models should pass safety testing before release, and that once weights are public, safeguards can't be added back.

Fact: Anthropic has never released an open-weight model. In a July 2026 position statement, CEO Dario Amodei wrote that Anthropic "has never advocated for a ban on open-weights models." Whether open weights become the dominant threat is an open question that no one can settle yet.

Epilogue

It was always about one file

Text became tokens. Tokens became vectors. Random knobs got nudged, trillions of times, until they captured something about language. Then the knobs froze into a checkpoint.

Everything after that, API or download, laptop or data center, Nvidia or custom silicon, is a decision about who gets to hold that file and who gets paid to run it.

Next time you read that a model is "open," you'll know to ask the better questions: open how, under what license, and running on whose hardware?
