Chapter 1

Where neoclouds fit

Where do neoclouds sit within the AI economy?
Energy and chips flow up into infrastructure. Infrastructure turns them into compute, which model builders rent to train and run AI.

Think of the AI economy as a five-story building. The bottom floors make raw ingredients: electricity and chips. The top floors make what you actually use: models and apps.

Floor three is where this story lives. These companies buy the chips, plug them into power, and rent out the result. That result is compute, the raw processing power to train and run models.

Wall Street splits the floor-three tenants into two camps. Hyperscalers are household names with other businesses behind them. Neoclouds are newer, GPU-focused specialists, and the source reel names CoreWeave and Nebius as the poster children.

Chapter 2

The AI factory

What does it physically take to produce AI compute?

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang likes to call these buildings AI factories. The analogy holds up: raw materials go in, a product comes out. The reel boils the recipe down to three inputs. Scroll, and we'll build one together.

Each step adds one physical input. The buttons above let you jump around.

⚡ Input 1 · Electrical energy

Power arrives from the grid at high voltage. A substation's transformers step it down, and switchgear routes it to the building. A single modern GPU rack can draw on the order of 100+ kilowatts, more than dozens of homes, so a campus is often limited by how much grid power it can secure.

▣ Input 2 · The powered shell

A "powered shell" is a building with the electrical and mechanical backbone already in place: power distribution, cooling, fiber, security. It's a warehouse wired to feed machines. Many neoclouds started as crypto miners and already had exactly this: cheap power and big sheds.

◆ Input 3 · GPU hardware

Racks of GPU servers are the engine. They're also the most expensive part and the fastest to age. Each rack is tied together with high-speed networking and usually liquid cooling, because packing this much power into one cabinet makes serious heat.

= Output · Compute

Combine all three and the factory produces compute capacity, delivered over fiber to customers who train and run models. In reality there's more on the list (networking gear, storage, staff, permits, water, software), so treat the three-input recipe as the skeleton, not the whole body.

Chapter 4

Hyperscalers vs. neoclouds

Why does financing look so different for established tech giants and neoclouds?

Microsoft sells Office. Amazon is the everything store. Google and Meta sell ads. Those businesses throw off enormous cash, and at first that cash paid for most of the AI build-out. As spending climbed, hyperscalers started borrowing too, but when a bank lends to Microsoft, it looks at debt against huge, steady earnings, and the borrowing looks modest.

A neocloud needs the same chips but has no cash cow to sell. It earns money from compute, yet it must spend billions before that revenue arrives. And its existing business often can't support the debt it needs on its own. So it leans much harder on equity and on borrowing secured by specific contracts and hardware.

Chapter 6

The SPV financing structure

How can contracted revenue and GPU collateral support borrowing?

Enter chip-backed financing. The idea: put the contract and the chips it pays for into a separate legal box, a special-purpose vehicle, and let lenders lend against that box alone. If the parent company stumbles, the box is walled off. Lenders are mainly underwriting the customer's ability to pay, not the neocloud's. As the reel puts it, the neocloud gets to borrow on Meta's credit instead of its own.

Financing X-ray
Structure

Scroll to X-ray the structure

Each step lights up one layer of the deal. The buttons let you switch layers or compare legal structures.

1 · Physical assets

The GPUs sit in a data center the neocloud operates, often in a leased powered shell. In structure A the SPV legally owns those servers even though the parent runs them day to day. CoreWeave's recent facilities use a bankruptcy-remote SPV set up to own the GPUs and act as borrower [4].

2 · Commercial contracts

The customer signs a services agreement. Depending on the deal, the contract (or the right to its payments) is assigned to the SPV, or simply pledged to lenders. The parent usually signs a management agreement to keep operating the hardware for the SPV.

3 · Debt & collateral

Lenders advance money to the SPV and take a security interest in what's inside: the GPUs and the contract rights. The debt is often non-recourse to the parent (CoreWeave describes its March 2026 facility that way [7]), so lenders' claim is on the box, not the whole company. Some facilities add parent guarantees. The parent contributes equity to cover what the loan doesn't.

4 · Cash movement

Customer payments land in a controlled collection account. A waterfall pays operating costs first, then interest and principal, and only what's left trickles up to the parent. That ordering is what makes lenders comfortable.

Illustrative structures Real facilities differ in who owns what, how contracts are assigned, reserve accounts and guarantees. Structure C reflects reporting on xAI's Colossus 2 financing, where a standalone vehicle buys GPUs and leases them to the operator [5].

Chapter 7

How lenders size the debt

How much can lenders advance against an expected stream of cash flows?

Here's the part people get wrong: a $500 million contract does not mean $500 million of loans. Lenders strip out costs, demand a safety cushion, and then ask what a stream of future payments is worth today. On the original whiteboard, that chain of logic turns $100 million a year into roughly $250 million of borrowing. Try it yourself.

Chapter 8

Buying the GPUs and selling compute

What happens after the financing closes?

The loan closes. "Voila, they sell compute," says the reel. In practice there's a production line between the signature and the first invoice. Follow the money, then the machines, then the money again.

Scroll to follow the money and the machines

Six stages, from loan closing to the first payment.

① Lenders fund, in installments

Most GPU facilities are delayed-draw term loans: money is released in tranches as hardware actually arrives, so nobody pays interest on idle cash. CoreWeave's March 2026 facility, for example, allowed about $7.5B to be drawn at first, growing to $8.5B as assets stabilized [3].

② GPUs are purchased

The cash goes straight to hardware vendors. For a sense of scale: to serve a ~$9.7B, five-year Microsoft contract, IREN agreed to buy about $5.8B of GPUs and related gear from Dell [10].

③ Equipment is installed

Servers are racked, cabled to high-speed networks and plumbed into liquid cooling, inside a powered shell that's either owned or leased.

④ Capacity goes live

After burn-in and acceptance testing, the cluster is handed to the customer. Contracts often tie payment start dates to these delivery milestones, which makes construction delays a financial risk.

⑤ Compute is delivered

The customer trains and runs models on reserved capacity, month after month, under service-level commitments.

⑥ The customer pays, and the waterfall runs

Payments land in the SPV's collection account, then flow down in strict order: rent and energy, then lenders, then whatever's left up to the parent.

Chapter 9

The profit squeeze

How do depreciation, interest and other costs affect profitability?

"While this might seem great," the reel warns, "the depreciation and interest eat away at much of the profit." Here's why. Those GPUs cost real money up front. Accounting spreads that cost over their useful life as depreciation, and the loan charges interest on top. Together they can swallow almost everything the contract earns.

Chapter 10

The web, and where it can tear

What happens when one contract becomes dozens, and what introduces risk?

"But that's just one contract and one SPV. In reality, there are many." Zoom out and a neocloud looks less like one factory and more like a constellation: a parent company orbited by financing vehicles, each tied to its own customer, its own chips and its own lenders.

Whiteboard illustration The original board draws one SPV each for Meta, Anthropic and OpenAI. That's a teaching map. Public filings don't name every customer behind every facility: CoreWeave's investment-grade facility was reportedly backed by its Meta contract [4], while a later facility cited two undisclosed non-investment-grade customers [6].

Epilogue

The central bank of AI?

How do Nvidia's commercial and financial ties support the neocloud ecosystem?

Every dollar a neocloud borrows ends up buying, mostly, Nvidia hardware. So Nvidia has a strong reason to keep its best customers alive, and it's tied to CoreWeave three ways at once.

Supplier. CoreWeave has bought hundreds of thousands of Nvidia GPUs [13].

Shareholder. Nvidia invested $2B in January 2026, bringing its stake to roughly 11% [14].

Backstop. A $6.3B order obliges Nvidia to buy CoreWeave's residual unsold capacity through April 13, 2032 [15]. That's a contingent commitment, which is why it's drawn dashed, and it's capped. CoreWeave stays on the hook for its own debt.

The reel says those ties help lower CoreWeave's borrowing costs and cites The Economist calling Nvidia "the central bank of AI" source claim. Nvidia is also a customer of Lambda: it agreed to lease back about 18,000 of its own GPUs for about $1.5B [16].

When the chip seller is also the investor and the buyer of last resort, the loop is strong in good times. It's worth asking what it looks like if demand ever turns.
