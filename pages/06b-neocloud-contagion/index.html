Neocloud Contagion Monitor

Neoclouds borrow heavily against chips and contracts, and their landlords borrow against the neoclouds. This page tracks that web live, then lets you push on it: what happens to each company if Nvidia, or the whole Nasdaq, falls hard? How much forced selling would follow, and at what point does the selling start feeding on itself?

6 · How the engine works

Assumptions, in plain sight

1 · Spreading the shock. Each stock's daily move is βNasdaq·(Nasdaq move) + βNvidia·(Nvidia-specific move) + its own shocks. The betas come from a two-factor regression on the last year of daily returns, recomputed every refresh. A drawdown spread over N days is applied as N equal compounding steps.

2 · Credit ties. Company-specific shocks (a CoreWeave credit event, hyperscalers pulling back) pass along the exposure weights in the table below, times the pass-through slider. When a company's model default risk tops 50% mid-scenario, half its shock passes on again (a second round).

3 · Margin calls. Margin debt in each name is the base share × a retail tilt of (1 − institutional ownership)/0.25, capped between 0.5× and 3×. Surviving margin accounts have loan/value ratios spread from 5% up to the "most-levered" setting, with more accounts at lower leverage. An account is called once its loan/value passes 1 − maintenance, and it sells down to 75% of its maintenance limit. A fully underwater account sells everything.

4 · Leveraged ETFs. Each 2x or 3x fund must trade L(L−1)·AUM·r every day it rebalances. AUM is live from Yahoo and shrinks as the fund loses money.

5 · Price impact. For a single stock, forced selling Q moves the price by Y·σdaily·√(Q/ADV), the square-root law used in market-impact research. In stress, average daily volume is taken as doubled. For the whole market, $1 of forced selling removes $M of value, following Gabaix & Koijen's "inelastic markets" estimate of roughly 5 (range 3–8). Short-run effects can be larger. The engine solves each day as a fixed point, because forced selling lowers prices, which forces more selling.

6 · Safety valves. Circuit breakers cap any single day's market drop at 20% and push the remainder into the next day. A backstop, meaning a central-bank or other lender-of-last-resort liquidity injection, absorbs 80% of forced selling once the market drawdown reaches its trigger.

7 · Default risk. This is a simplified Merton model with a one-year horizon: equity is treated as an option on the company's assets, using its market value, total debt, and 3-month volatility. In stress, volatility is scaled up by (1 + |drawdown|). It's a ranking tool, not a rating, and Yahoo's total-debt figure includes leases.

What the engine leaves out: options-dealer hedging, hedge-fund deleveraging, debt covenant triggers, refinancing needs, and how GPU resale prices tie to Nvidia's stock. Each of these could make a real cascade bigger or smaller than shown. Not investment advice.
