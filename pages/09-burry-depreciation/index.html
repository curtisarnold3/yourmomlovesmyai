Burry & the AI Depreciation Question

How GPU useful life changes earnings, valuations, and the economics of AI infrastructure

Picture two server racks, bolt for bolt identical. Same chips, same cooling, same $100 billion price tag. One accountant writes "5 years" on the first. Another writes "2 years" on the second. Nothing about the metal changes, and yet one company suddenly reports far higher profit. Is that a trick, a judgment call, or something that barely matters? Let's follow the money and find out.

CHAPTER 01

Burry's depreciation argument

Are hyperscalers assuming AI chips will last longer than their true economic useful lives?

Allegation · Nov 10, 2025

Michael Burry — the investor portrayed in The Big Short — broke a two-year silence on X in late October 2025. On November 10 he went after something most people skip: the depreciation footnote.

Understating depreciation by extending useful lives is “one of the more common frauds of the modern era.”

His paraphrased case: hyperscalers buy Nvidia chips on a 2–3 year product cycle yet depreciate servers over 5–6 years. He estimated roughly $176B of understated depreciation across 2026–2028, singling out Oracle (earnings overstated ~27% by 2028, by his math) and Meta (~21%), with Alphabet, Microsoft and Amazon in his table. These are his estimates and opinions, not findings by any regulator or auditor.

Same metal, different label

Here's the strange part. Look at the two racks — they're identical. The only thing that differs is a number in a spreadsheet: how many years the company assumes the hardware will keep earning money.

Spread $100B over 5 years and you book $20B a year. Spread it over 2 and you book $50B. Same purchase, different yearly expense.

Keep two ideas apart from here on. The book life is the label in the footnote: an accounting estimate. The economic life is how long the chips actually earn their keep before they must be replaced. Changing the label alone moves reported profit; changing the economics moves cash (Chapters 7–10).

Verified What companies disclosed

The lives really did move. Microsoft lengthened server and network equipment from 4 to 6 years (fiscal 2023). Alphabet did the same for servers in 2023. Oracle went 5 → 6 in fiscal 2025. Meta moved most servers to 5.5 years in January 2025.

Amazon is the interesting one. It had extended servers from 5 to 6 years in January 2024 — then in January 2025 cut a subset of servers and networking equipment back to 5 years, citing the faster pace of AI and machine-learning technology. It now reports a 5–6 year range.

Allegation ≠ proven violation

Under U.S. GAAP, a useful life is a management estimate of how long an asset will provide economic benefit. Revising it is a change in accounting estimate, applied going forward — not an accounting error, and not fraud by itself, provided the original estimate was reasonable given what was known at the time.

There's evidence on both sides. CoreWeave's CEO said a customer re-contracted H100s within 5% of the original price, and CoreWeave has signed A100 (2020-era) capacity into 2029 — though as a GPU landlord CoreWeave has its own stake in long chip lives. So the real question is narrower and harder: are these assumptions reasonable and supported?

CHAPTER 02

How stock valuations work

How do investors use earnings to estimate a stock's value?

Source analogy

Your neighbor's house just sold for $500 a square foot. Yours is 2,000 square feet. Without hiring anyone, you can ballpark your home at $1,000,000.

That's a "comp": borrow a price from something similar, multiply by how much of it you have.

From houses to companies

Stocks get the same treatment. Instead of price per square foot, investors often use the price-to-earnings multiple. Instead of square feet, they use earnings per share.

Implied share price = P/E multiple × EPS

The reel sketched a comps chart for the big cloud companies averaging about 22× earnings. Those hand-drawn multiples weren't dated or sourced, so we don't reuse them as data — multiples move every trading day.

The catch

EPS is an accounting number. Change the depreciation assumption and EPS moves — and if you hold the multiple fixed at, say, 20×, the implied price moves with it: $6.00 → $120, $3.75 → $75.

Keep it honest

A multiple is not a law of physics. Markets can (and often do) look through accounting choices and apply a different multiple to lower-quality earnings. The calculator below shows one valuation approach — not a guaranteed market price.

CHAPTER 03

The $100 billion GPU purchase

What happens financially when a company buys $100 billion of AI equipment?

Day one: cash leaves

The checks clear. $100B of real cash leaves the company to pay for GPUs, trays, racks and networking. This is the moment money actually moves.

Assumption: paid in cash at the start of Year 1 — no debt, leases or vendor financing.

Placed in service

The racks are installed and switched on. On the balance sheet, cash goes down by $100B and property & equipment goes up by $100B. Total assets? Unchanged on day one. The company swapped one asset for another.

Into the accounting records

Why not expense all $100B on day one? Because the racks will help earn revenue for several years. Accounting tries to match the cost to the years that benefit, so each year carries a share. That process is depreciation: part of the $100B moves from the balance sheet to the income statement as an expense each year.

Depreciation spreads a payment that already happened. It does not send the money out a second time.

On the cash-flow statement

The $100B shows up exactly once, as capital expenditure in investing activities. Every later year, depreciation is added back when computing operating cash flow, because no cash moved.

Explicit assumptions: $100B paid in cash at start of Year 1; placed in service immediately; full-year depreciation in Year 1; no financing; 100% bonus tax depreciation; $100B revenue; 25% tax.

CHAPTER 04

Five years vs. two years

How does the useful life change the annual expense?

Same starting point

Both timelines begin with the same $100B asset, bought with the same $100B of cash (the amber bar). Watch only one thing change: how the cost is spread.

Scenario A · 5 years
$100B ÷ 5 = $20B per year

A thin, even slice each year for five years.

Scenario B · 2 years
$100B ÷ 2 = $50B per year

Thicker slices, finished in two years. Years 3–5 carry no depreciation for this asset at all.

The total never changes

5 × $20B = 2 × $50B = $100B. Useful life changes when the cost hits earnings — not how much in total.

A useful life is an accounting estimate, not a promised failure date. The hardware may keep running after it's fully depreciated, or be retired before.

CHAPTER 05

The effect on earnings

How does a depreciation assumption change EPS?

Source example

A simplified hyperscaler: $100B revenue, the $100B of chips, no other operating costs, a 25% tax assumption and 10B shares. Deliberately unrealistic — so we can see one lever at a time.

Subtract depreciation

Five-year life: $100B − $20B = $80B pretax. Two-year life: $100B − $50B = $50B pretax.

Subtract illustrative tax (25%)

$80B × 25% = $20B → net income $60B. $50B × 25% = $12.5B → net income $37.5B.

Teaching convention: tax is taken on the book earnings line. Real cash taxes follow separate tax books — Chapter 6.

Divide by 10B shares

EPS $6.00 vs EPS $3.75 — a 37.5% drop, from one assumption in one footnote. That's the core of Burry's earnings argument.

CHAPTER 06

Two sets of books

Why can financial statements and tax records show different depreciation?

One asset, two records

"Two sets of books" sounds shady. It isn't. Every large U.S. company keeps financial statements for investors and a separate tax return for the government — and they're supposed to differ.

Financial statements · goal: transparency

Management estimates a useful life and (commonly) uses straight-line depreciation so investors see a steady, comparable expense. Not every company or asset must use straight-line, but it's the norm for servers.

Tax books · goal: set policy Verified law

Congress writes the tax depreciation rules. The 2025 law (P.L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualified property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025.

Not automatic for everything: property acquired earlier falls under the old phase-down (40% in 2025), taxpayers can elect out, and eligibility rules apply. Book useful life plays no role in this deduction. Property bought under a written binding contract signed before Jan 20, 2025 counts as acquired before that date, even if delivered later. Several states (e.g. California, New York) don't follow federal bonus depreciation, so some state cash tax is usually still due. Simplified here: the mid-quarter and disposal-year conventions.

The timing gap → deferred tax

Year 1: book tax expense is $20B, but the tax return deducts all $100B, so cash tax on this business is $0. The $20B difference becomes a deferred tax liability — tax postponed, not forgiven. It unwinds as book depreciation catches up.

CHAPTER 07

Depreciation vs. actual cash flow

Does changing book depreciation make more cash leave the company?

Two tracks

Top track: what the income statement reports. Bottom track: what the bank account actually does. Same $100B purchase underneath both.

Accounting earnings differ

Five-year life: $60B net income every year. Two-year life: $37.5B in years 1–2, then $75B in years 3–5 once the chips are fully depreciated. Same lifetime total, different timing.

Cash flow doesn't

Operating cash flow is identical in every year under both lives: $100B, then $75B, $75B, $75B, $75B. Revenue is the same, cash taxes come from the tax books, and depreciation is noncash.

Why: the reconciliation
OCF = NI + depreciation + deferred tax − ΔWC

Add back the noncash charge, add back tax expensed but not paid, and every book-life choice washes out.

Nuance

The reel says the life assumption has "no impact whatsoever" on cash flows. More precisely: changing only the book schedule doesn't repeat or change the cash purchase. Cash can change if tax depreciation were tied to it, if the hardware really must be replaced sooner, or through financing terms, covenants and investor reactions tied to reported earnings.

FCF = operating cash flow − capital expenditures (+ hardware sale proceeds). A common simplified presentation; definitions differ across companies and analysts.

CHAPTER 08

Discounted cash flow

How does an investor value a business using future cash flows?

Source Excel model

The reel calls this the gold standard: project the cash a business will generate, then discount it back to today. Its sketch uses operating cash flows of 100, 110, 120, 130, 140 (example units — not billions).

Money later is worth less
PV(FCFt) = FCFt ÷ (1 + r)t

At a 10% discount rate, 140 received in year 5 is worth about 87 today. Later boxes shrink as they travel back.

The question marks

Year 1 capex is the (100) chip purchase. Years 2–5 are drawn as "?". That's the whole debate: how often must the chips be bought again? The visual fills in one possible answer — re-buying every 2 years — and the value drops. The 10-K's useful life doesn't enter a DCF directly, but it likely informs many investors' capex assumptions.

Enterprise vs. equity

Businesses don't stop after year 5, so most DCFs add a terminal value: the value of all later years, often a steady cash flow growing at a disclosed rate g, worth FCF × (1 + g) ÷ (r − g). It is frequently the biggest piece of the answer — and the most assumption-heavy.

A DCF of free cash flow gives enterprise value. Subtract net debt and other claims to get equity value; divide by shares for a per-share estimate. It's still a model output built on assumptions — not the stock price.

CHAPTER 09

How long do AI chips really last?

Is accounting useful life the same as economic useful life?

Three different "lives"

Physical life: how long it powers on. Accounting life: the estimate in the footnote. Economic life: how long it earns more than it costs to run, compared with buying something newer. A GPU can be alive, depreciating, and still economically obsolete — or fully depreciated and still printing money.

What drives economic life

Performance per watt versus the next generation, electricity and cooling costs, the rental price per GPU-hour, demand for training versus inference, failure rates and resale value. Nvidia has moved to roughly annual architecture launches, which pressures the frontier.

Evidence both ways

Amazon, after lengthening server lives to 6 years in 2024, shortened a subset of servers and networking gear back to 5 in 2025, citing faster AI progress. Yet CoreWeave (which rents out GPUs, so it's not a neutral party) reported strong demand for older chips, including an H100 re-contract near its original price. Neither proves a universal answer — and we won't invent one.

Second careers

Chips that fall off the frontier for training can be redeployed to inference or lighter workloads, earning less but still earning. When book value sits far above what the hardware can still earn, that gap can eventually surface as accelerated depreciation, write-downs or losses on retirement.

CHAPTER 10

Does it change the stock's value?

When does a depreciation assumption change reported valuation — versus actual economic value?

P/E pathway

Longer book life → lower depreciation → higher EPS. Hold the multiple fixed, and the implied price rises. This is the channel Burry worries about: reported earnings flattering valuations.

At default settings, why is the P/E bar so much taller than the DCF bar? Two reasons: a 20× multiple implicitly bakes in growth, while this DCF assumes zero growth at a 10% discount rate (roughly 10× steady cash flow); and the DCF counts the $100B purchase itself as a cash outflow in year 1, which P/E on earnings never does. Compare how each bar moves between scenarios, not their heights.

Scenario A · book life stretched, economics unchanged

Set the book life at 6 years while the hardware really earns for 5. Year-1 EPS rises to $6.25; P/E value rises. DCF value? Unchanged — not a single dollar of cash moved.

GAAP doesn't let the excess sit there: once it's clear the chips last 5 years, the remaining cost is spread over the shorter remaining life (a change in estimate), so later EPS dips a little. A life known to be too long from day one would be an error, not an estimate.

Scenario B · economic life also shortens

Now suppose the chips truly must be replaced every 2 years, though the company started at a 5-year book life. Year-1 EPS looks exactly the same — but once the shorter life is evident, the remaining $80B is expensed in year 2 (EPS $1.50), new purchases use a 2-year life, capex triples over five years, and DCF value falls sharply. This is where the economics bite.

Scenario C · redeployment

Same 2-year frontier cycle, but retired chips run inference for 2 more years, contributing 25% of frontier revenue after their own power and running costs (an illustrative assumption; it ignores the power capacity they tie up). Much of the lost value comes back. Second lives matter.

The reveal

Back to our two identical racks. One purchase can produce different depreciation schedules, different EPS and different P/E values — with the same immediate cash outlay. What ultimately decides value is how long the hardware actually earns and how often it must be bought again.

The takeaway
Depreciation changes how capital investment appears in reported earnings. But the economic value of an AI infrastructure business ultimately depends on the cash it can generate, the equipment it must replace, and the assumptions investors make about the future.

Extending the accounting life of AI chips can increase reported earnings in the near term. Understanding the underlying business means looking past the depreciation schedule — to cash flow, actual hardware productivity, replacement cycles and future capital spending. Next time you see "useful life" in a 10-K footnote, you might read it a little differently.
