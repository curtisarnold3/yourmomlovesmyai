Chapter 2

Meet the loop

Picture a chipmaker that writes a check to a customer so the customer can buy the chipmaker's chips. Fine so far. Companies invest in customers all the time. Now add a neocloud (a newer company that rents out racks of AI chips) that borrows against that customer's contract, a bank that lends to the neocloud, and a promise from the chipmaker to cover the rent if the customer stumbles. Same dollars, many name tags. (A hyperscaler, in the filters below, is one of the giant cloud companies such as Microsoft, Amazon, Google or Meta.) Below is every deal we could source. Some lines are cash that moved. Plenty are only promises. The task is telling them apart.

The bull reading

Backers point to the fact that most of these lines are written contracts, often with large, well-resourced customers who committed to years of demand before the buildings were finished. They argue that a chipmaker holding a stake in its customers shares their upside, and they point to reports that spending by end users has been growing.

The bear reading

Skeptics point to how many of these lines run back to a small set of names, so that one dollar can show up as an investment, as someone else's revenue and as a backlog number. They note that the dashed lines are promises rather than cash, and they warn that if end users spend less than hoped, many of these lines could weaken together because they lean on the same future spending.

What this means. Read the solid lines and the dashed lines as two different maps. Solid is money that moved. Dashed is an agreement that might. The loop is real. How much of it is paid for by outsiders is the question we'll chase through the rest of this page, and we don't know the full answer yet.
