Who has a claim on one GPU?

The claims stack

Picture one GPU sitting in a rack. Now count the people who think they have a claim on it.

A lender took it as collateral. A bank lent to the lender. Bond holders at the parent company are watching it too. The customer prepaid for its time. A big supplier promised to cover the rent if the customer walks. Somebody owns the equity, and somebody else owns a piece of the customer.

Same chip. Seven layers of paperwork (some of chapter 3's nine claims are grouped here, so you see seven). Let's stack them up and see what happens when the chip gets older. (Toy numbers throughout, per $100 of seed cash.)

This is not double-pledging.

Double-pledging means promising the same thing to two different lenders without telling them. That is not what is happening here, and nothing on this page implies it. Layered claims are an ordinary capital structure. Your house can carry a mortgage, a home-equity line and a contractor's lien, and nobody calls that fraud. Each layer has its own place in line, and everyone can read where.

The question here is different. Nearly every layer gets paid from the same place: future spending by end users on AI. When claims all depend on one thing, they tend to wobble together. That's the point of this chapter, and it's a point about correlation, not wrongdoing.
