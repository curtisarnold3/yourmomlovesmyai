Chapter 7

Who's on both sides

Imagine your landlord is also your lender, your biggest tenant and a part-owner of your business. In AI infrastructure that is called a partnership, and it cuts two ways: it lines up interests, and it ties fates together. When two companies sit on several sides of the same table at once, a problem at one of them doesn't stay put. We went through every deal in our dataset and lined up each pair of companies by the roles they play for each other.

    The bull reading

    Backers point to how long-term infrastructure usually gets built: a supplier that also invests is putting its own money behind its product, and a customer that owns a piece has a reason to stay. They note that cloud, telecom and shipping all grew up with ties like these, and that many of these links are written into contracts that lenders and auditors review.

    The bear reading

    Skeptics point to how a stumble can travel: when one company is another's supplier, customer and investor at once, trouble at one can pass along all three links in the same week. They add that the ties make it harder to tell how much demand is real, since some of a customer's spending may trace back to its supplier's own money, a share that can't be measured precisely from public deals.

    What this means. One company wearing several hats is not a scandal, and it isn't proof of a bubble either. It's a map of how much would have to go right together. The more roles two firms play for each other, the more their fortunes are one story, and that's the thing to watch when the weather changes.
